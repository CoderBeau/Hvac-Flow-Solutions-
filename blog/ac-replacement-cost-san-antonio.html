Most San Antonio homeowners pay between $8,000 and $14,000 to replace a complete AC system in 2026. A basic 2–3 ton builder-grade replacement can land near $5,500–$9,000. A high-efficiency variable-speed system in a larger home can reach $20,000 or more.

That spread is real, not a dodge — and the rest of this page explains exactly what moves a quote from one end of it to the other, so you can tell a fair number from a padded one.

Prices moved recently

Quotes in 2026 are running roughly $1,500 to $3,000 higher than the same job two seasons ago, driven by equipment cost increases and the refrigerant transition. If you're comparing against a number a neighbor paid in 2023, it will look wrong. It isn't.

Why two quotes for the same house differ by $6,000

This is the question behind the question. Six variables account for nearly all of the spread:

  1. Tonnage. Size is driven by square footage, ceiling height, insulation, and window exposure — not by what's currently installed. Plenty of San Antonio homes are running a unit that was sized wrong to begin with.
  2. Efficiency tier. Every step up in SEER2 adds equipment cost. It also cuts your summer bills, which matters more here than almost anywhere else — see below.
  3. Single-stage vs. two-stage vs. variable-speed. Higher stages cost more and run longer at lower power, which pulls more humidity out of the air. In a South Texas August that's a comfort difference you can feel, not just a spec.
  4. Ductwork. The single biggest swing factor. A contractor who inspects your ducts and finds them leaking will quote higher than one who never looked — and be right to.
  5. Electrical and line-set work. Older homes often need a new disconnect, a breaker change, or a replacement line set. Legitimate, and easy to hide in a vague quote.
  6. Brand and warranty. Premium brands cost more up front. The install quality matters more than the badge — a well-installed mid-tier system outlasts a sloppy premium one.

The San Antonio factor: your system works harder than most

National cost guides assume a cooling season of three or four months. Ours runs from March into October, with long stretches above 100°F. A San Antonio air conditioner accumulates far more runtime hours per year than the same unit in Denver or Chicago.

Three consequences worth pricing in:

  • Efficiency pays back faster here. The extra cost of a higher SEER2 tier is recovered over more cooling hours than the national math assumes. Stepping up is a more defensible spend in Bexar County than it is in most of the country.
  • Undersizing shows up immediately. A unit that's marginally small will keep up in May and lose the house in August. Insist on a load calculation, not a guess based on the old unit's label.
  • Lifespan runs shorter. Systems that might see 15–20 years in a mild climate often need replacement closer to 12–15 here. If yours is past 12 and facing a major repair, replacement math starts to win.

Don't forget heat

February 2021 taught everyone in this city that heating is not a rounding error. If you're considering a heat pump — and the rebates favor them — ask specifically how the system handles a hard freeze and what the backup heat strip capacity is. A heat pump sized purely for cooling can struggle in a Texas cold snap without adequate auxiliary heat.

Rebates and tax credits in 2026 — what changed

The federal credit is gone

The Section 25C energy efficiency tax credit — the one worth up to $2,000 on a qualifying heat pump — was eliminated by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act signed July 4, 2025. There is no federal residential heat pump tax credit in 2026. If a contractor's quote still shows one, the quote is out of date, and that tells you something about how closely they're paying attention.

What is still available locally is the CPS Energy STEP rebate, and it's meaningful:

Note how the rebate interacts with tier choice: moving from 15.9 to 16 SEER2 swings the rebate by $75 per ton. On a 4-ton system that's $300 back, which can substantially offset the equipment upcharge. Ask your contractor to quote the tier just above the threshold and compare.

The rules your contractor has to follow

Minimum efficiency: 14.3 SEER2

Since January 1, 2023, the U.S. Department of Energy has required a minimum of 14.3 SEER2 for split-system air conditioners installed in the South region, which includes all of Texas. Equipment below that minimum cannot legally be installed here. If someone offers you a suspiciously cheap unit, ask for the SEER2 rating in writing.

Permits

The City of San Antonio requires a permit for HVAC replacement. It's not bureaucratic box-ticking — it's the check that the electrical work is safe, gas connections are sound, and the system meets current energy code. Unpermitted work can surface as a problem years later during a home sale, and it's a genuine reason to walk away from a bid.

Ask for this in writing

A quote you can actually compare should name: tonnage, SEER2 rating, model numbers for both indoor and outdoor units, whether a load calculation was performed, permit included yes/no, ductwork scope, labor warranty length, and the equipment warranty registration terms. Any quote missing three or more of those isn't a quote — it's a number.

Repair or replace?

A rough rule that holds up well in this climate: if the repair costs more than a third of a replacement and the system is past 10 years old, replacement usually wins. Specific situations that tilt it further:

  • The compressor failed and the unit is out of warranty. Compressor replacement on an aging system is rarely worth it.
  • The system uses R-22. That refrigerant is no longer produced. Recharges are expensive and getting worse.
  • It's the second significant repair in two seasons. The pattern matters more than any single failure.
  • Rooms have never cooled evenly. That's usually sizing or ductwork, and a like-for-like swap won't fix it.

Conversely, a single capacitor, contactor, or blower motor failure on a well-maintained 8-year-old system is a repair, not a reason to spend five figures. A contractor who reaches for replacement on a system that young should be asked to justify it.

Frequently asked questions

How much does it cost to replace an AC unit in San Antonio?

Most San Antonio homeowners pay $8,000–$14,000 for a complete system replacement in 2026. Entry-level 2–3 ton systems start around $5,500, while high-efficiency variable-speed systems in larger homes can exceed $20,000. Ductwork replacement adds $3,000–$8,000 when needed.

Is there a tax credit for a new AC in 2026?

No federal one. The Section 25C credit was eliminated by legislation signed in July 2025, so there is no federal residential heat pump tax credit in 2026. CPS Energy's STEP rebate is still available locally, worth $100–$275 per ton depending on the SEER2 rating.

How long does an AC last in San Antonio?

Typically 12–15 years, shorter than the 15–20 years often quoted nationally. Our cooling season runs March through October with long stretches above 100°F, so local systems accumulate substantially more runtime hours per year than the national average assumes.

Do I need a permit to replace my AC in San Antonio?

Yes. The City of San Antonio requires a permit for HVAC replacement, covering electrical safety, gas connections, and energy code compliance. A licensed contractor handles this as part of the job. Unpermitted work can cause complications when you sell the home.

What size AC do I need for my home?

Size should come from a load calculation that accounts for square footage, ceiling height, insulation, window exposure, and orientation — not from the tonnage of your existing unit, which may have been wrong from the start. As a very rough starting point, San Antonio homes often need about one ton per 500–600 square feet, but treat that as a sanity check on a contractor's number, never a substitute for the calculation.

How many quotes should I get?

Three. One quote gives you no reference point; two leaves you guessing which is the outlier. Three lets you see the pattern, and the spread itself is informative — if one bid is dramatically lower, it usually means something was left out of the scope.
